The Moon from Earth

—

The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon circles Earth, we see more or less of that lit half. That's what makes the phases. Earth's shadow has nothing to do with it. (Seen from the Northern Hemisphere; down south it's flipped.)

Earth's shadow and eclipses

—

Looking from Earth straight down its shadow. The dark circle is the umbra (full shadow) and the lighter ring is the penumbra (part shadow). The Moon only turns red when it slides into the umbra.

Why not every full moon? The Moon's path is tilted about 5°, so most months it passes above or below the shadow. In the 3D view the Moon's distance is squeezed and its tilt is stretched by the same amount, so it still lines up with the shadow the way it really does.

    How much junk is up there?

    This view shows a sample of about 4,800 objects in realistic orbits, not their exact positions today. In real life, trackers follow about 40,000 objects bigger than a softball. Scientists estimate there are over a million pieces between 1 and 10 cm, plus many millions of smaller bits.

    When pieces crash, they break into more pieces. If that keeps snowballing, it's called the Kessler syndrome. That's why space agencies now try to bring old satellites down.
